2d:T90d,# Master Fatai: The Sutra That Fell into the River and Did Not Get Wet

> Recorded in the "Eminent Monks" section of the *Xianying lu* under "Master Tai of Meizhou", and again in the Sui section of the *Chiyan ji* under "The monk Fatai of Sui". The two versions differ only in wording. The English rendering follows the *Chiyan ji*.

## The Story

Master Fatai, a native of Longshan in Meizhou, constantly recited the Lotus Sutra and had copied out a scroll of it with his own hand; responses and signs had appeared more than once.

Once he sent the completed scroll to Yizhou to be mounted; on the way it fell from the Zao bridge into the water below, and no search could find it. Fatai wept in grief, walking up and down along the bank. Suddenly he caught sight of a wrapped bundle on a small islet, and ordered it fetched — it was the sutra: grass and branches held it up, and there was not so much as one wet spot upon it.

Overcome with joy, Fatai at once had the scroll taken to Chengdu and finished, mounted on sandalwood rollers, adorned with every solemnity, and escorted back for offering in his home monastery. From then on, an extraordinary fragrance filled the room every night. He redoubled his recitation, reading the sutra through once every night without fail.

At that time a Master Biao was lecturing in the monastery. One evening he walked to the place where Fatai recited, and saw a multitude of people kneeling with palms joined, gathered around listening — Biao broke into a sweat and withdrew.

## Editor's Reflection

This is a "physical evidence" type of response record: the scroll itself is the protagonist. Copying, mounting, falling into the water, recovery, dryness, fragrance, nightly recitation, the unseen audience — every link is perceptible and checkable. The compiler's intent is to declare the scroll's inviolable sacredness ("held by grass and branches, without a single wet spot") and the reciter's power to draw unseen protectors ("the crowd kneeling with joined palms" — the "invisible assembly listening to the sutra", a stock image of the genre).

Incidentally, the entry is a charming slice of book history: copy → mount → sandalwood rollers → enshrine in the home monastery — the complete production process of a deluxe sutra volume from before the Tang.
