18:T7d3,# Zhang Qing: Care for Prisoners as Part of Daily Responsibility

> This editorial rendering follows the cited source. Extraordinary events are reported as religious narratives, not independently established facts.

## The account

The Chiyan ji records Zhang Qing, a prison official in Bianjing during the Northern Song Xiangfu era. It says that he conducted himself with compassion and caution. He attended to the cleanliness of prisoners’ food, medicines, and bedding, taking particular care during the hot months.

He reminded his colleagues that people caught by the criminal law were already unfortunate: if those responsible for them showed no concern, where could they turn with their complaints? Zhang himself recited the Lotus Sutra throughout his life. Whenever a prisoner faced execution, he kept a vegetarian observance and recited on that person’s behalf for a month. He also urged prisoners to acknowledge their own offences without falsely accusing innocent people.

The same entry relates that his wife, Lady Yuan, died of an epidemic illness at forty-eight and revived three days later. She described a white-robed sacred figure leading her away from a polluted place and speaking of Zhang’s hidden virtue and future children. The subsequent birth of a son, the official careers of descendants, and Zhang’s peaceful death at eighty-two belong to the text’s continuing religious narrative. They do not establish promises of children, office, or longevity in return for good conduct.

The clearest everyday emphasis is Zhang’s treatment of people under his authority. Prisoners’ food, medicine, cleanliness, and need to be heard remained matters of care. The text places his recitation alongside these responsibilities.

## Editorial reflection (not source text)

Work can offer opportunities to attend to people whose basic needs are easily overlooked. Present-day duties must still meet legal and professional standards; religious practice cannot replace practical care.
