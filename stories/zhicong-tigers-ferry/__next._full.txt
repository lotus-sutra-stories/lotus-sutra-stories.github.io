2d:T9d9,# Master Zhicong: Four Tigers Cross with Him; He Passes Away Seated

> Recorded in the "Eminent Monks" section of the *Xianying lu* under "Master Cong of Yangzhou", and again in the Tang section of the *Chiyan ji* under "The monk Zhicong of Qixia Monastery on Mount She". The *Xianying lu* version alone carries the episode of offering his body to the tigers — its most moving element. The English rendering follows the *Xianying lu*.

## The Story

The monk Zhicong lived at White Horse Monastery in Yangzhou, devoted to the study of the Three Treatises. Later he crossed the river and settled at Anle Monastery.

When the Sui dynasty collapsed, he longed to return home but could find no way; so he hid among the reeds by the river and recited the Lotus Sutra day after day. For seven days he ate nothing and felt no hunger. Around him four tigers constantly kept gentle guard.

(The *Xianying lu* adds:) Zhicong said to the tigers: "I have not eaten for ten days; my life hangs on a breath. If you are hungry, then eat me." The four tigers kept watch, shedding tears, and would not leave.

Suddenly an old man came poling a little boat, saying: "Master, let me ferry you across." Zhicong boarded; the four tigers crouched on the bank and would not depart. He asked: "Do you too wish to cross?" The tigers rose at once, crossed the water together in safety, and reached the south bank. When he looked back, the little boat and the old man had vanished.

Thereafter Zhicong kept the four tigers with him on the west of Qixia Monastery on Mount She. In the Zhenguan era, ninety-nine years old, he died seated upright; the tigers went away with mournful roars.

## Editor's Reflection

The most moving episode is the one only the *Xianying lu* preserves — the offering of his body to the tigers: a monk seven days without food gives his body to the hungry beasts that guard him. The gesture inverts the usual structure of "recite and be saved": the one protected becomes the giver, and the protectors become the recipients. "The four tigers shed tears and would not leave" are the weightiest eight characters in the whole account.

The closing line — "the boat and the old man had vanished" — quietly settles the ferryman's identity: that a single small skiff should appear at just that moment on a war-torn river is itself the answer. In the editor's reading, the real "response" here is neither the tigers nor the boat, but the composure of a man who in a collapsing world still "felt no hunger for seven days".
