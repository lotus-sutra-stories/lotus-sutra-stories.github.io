18:T79c,# Master Huiqing: In the Storm, Hands That Seemed to Hold the Boat

> Recorded in the "Eminent Monks" section of the *Xianying lu* under "Master Qing of Mount Lu" (citing the *Fayuan zhulin*), and again in the Song section of the *Chiyan ji* under "The monk Huiqing of the Song". The English rendering draws on both versions.

## The Story

The monk Huiqing was a native of Guangling. In the Yuanjia era of the Liu Song he lived on Mount Lu. He had mastered both the sutras and the Vinaya, and his observance of the precepts was pure and exacting.

Whenever he recited the Lotus Sutra (together with the Sutra of the Inquiring of Benefits, says the *Chiyan ji*) through the night, he would hear, in the darkness, the snap of fingers applauding him.

Once, crossing the river, he ran into a great wind, and the boat was within moments of capsizing. Huiqing single-mindedly recited the sutra, asking only for safe passage. He felt the boat, amid the waves, meet the gale head-on and cut through the racing current as though someone were pulling and holding it — and in the space of a glance, it reached the bank.

From that time on he never wearied of recitation, persisting into old age.

## Editor's Reflection

"The snap of fingers in the dark" is the most delicate touch in this account: in the vocabulary of the response literature, a finger-snap is the sound of unseen protectors signalling approval — the quiet encouragement of ordinary nights preceding the hidden assistance in mortal danger, the two echoing like an acquaintance who has long since made himself known.

Equally telling is the source's wording: "felt as if there were those who held the boat". Not "saw" — everyone aboard was amid the tumult and the praying, and nobody saw anything; the boat simply became light, became steady, pointed the right way. This kind of "somatic" narration sits closer to the practitioner's own world of experience than any apparition could.
