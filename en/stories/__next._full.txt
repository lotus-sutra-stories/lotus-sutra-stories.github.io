The source cites the Xu Gaoseng zhuan.30:Tc64,# The Monk Never Disparaging: "I Dare Not Disparage You"

> This account comes from Chapter 20 of the Lotus Sutra itself — a narrative of the remote past — and is transcribed in the "Ancient Sages" (古聖) section of the *Xianying lu*. It is scriptural, not a later historical record. The modern English rendering below follows the passage as excerpted in that compilation.

## The Story

In the semblance-dharma age that followed the parinirvāṇa of the Tathāgata Bhīṣmagarjitasvara ("Thunderous Voice of the Fearless King"), there were monks overcome with pride, enjoying great influence. In that age lived one monk named Never Disparaging (Sadāparibhūta).

He did not occupy himself chiefly with reciting the scriptures; his single practice was reverence. Whenever he saw any of the four assemblies — monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen — he would bow and praise them, saying:

> "I revere you deeply and dare not disparage you. You are all practising the bodhisattva path, and you shall all become Buddhas."

Among the assembly were those whose minds were impure and who grew angry. They reviled him harshly: "Where does this ignorant monk come from, declaring 'I dare not disparage you' and handing us these baseless prophecies? We want none of them!" Some struck him with staves and threw tiles and stones at him. He would withdraw to a distance to avoid them, yet still call out loudly: "I dare not disparage you. You shall all become Buddhas." Because he constantly said this, he came to be called Never Disparaging.

As that monk was about to die, there in open space he fully heard the Lotus Sutra as previously proclaimed by the Buddha Bhīṣmagarjitasvara; he was able to receive and retain it all, and attained purity of the six sense faculties. He then widely taught the sutra to others.

Śākyamuni Buddha told the assembly: that monk of that time was none other than myself. Had I not, in previous lives, received, retained, recited and taught this sutra for others, I could not have so quickly attained supreme awakening.

## Editor's Reflection

What Never Disparaging practised was complete reverence for the capacity for awakening in every living being. The sutra does not portray him as a wonder-worker; it records only one sentence and a lifetime of bows. Even when his contemporaries thought him mad, and struck and cursed him, the view "you shall all become Buddhas" never wavered. For a modern reader the story suggests that the seed of any "response" (感應) may lie not in reciting more scrolls, but in first learning not to disparage a single person.

As told in the Lotus Sutra: in the semblance-dharma age a monk bowed to everyone he met, saying "I dare not disparage you, for you shall all become Buddhas." Though mocked and stoned, he never altered his practice; at the end of his life he heard the Lotus Sutra proclaimed in open space and attained purity of the six senses. The Buddha identifies that monk as his own former self.
The monk Never Disparaging (a previous incarnation of Śākyamuni Buddha)

常不輕 不輕菩薩 Sadāparibhūta Never Disparaging31:Tbde,# Samantabhadra's Exhortation: Four Dharmas for Obtaining the Sutra

> This account is from the final chapter of the Lotus Sutra (Chapter 28, "Encouragement of Samantabhadra"), transcribed in the "Ancient Sages" section of the *Xianying lu*. The modern English rendering below follows the passage as excerpted in that compilation.

## The Story

Then the Bodhisattva Samantabhadra, arriving from the east, said to the Buddha: "In the buddha-land of Jewel-Majesty-Virtue-Sovereign-King in the east I heard from afar that this Lotus Sutra was being taught in this Sahā world, and I have come with a great host of bodhisattvas to hear and receive it. May the World-Honoured One proclaim it for beings. After the Tathāgata's passing, how shall beings come to obtain this sutra?"

The Buddha told Samantabhadra: "If a person accomplishes four dharmas, then after the Tathāgata's passing they will obtain this Lotus Sutra: first, to be watched over and protected by the buddhas; second, to have planted the roots of many virtues; third, to have entered the assembly of those firmly set on the right path; and fourth, to have conceived the resolve to rescue all living beings. One who accomplishes these four dharmas will surely obtain this sutra."

Samantabhadra said to the Buddha: "In the last five-hundred-year age, when the world is corrupted and turbid, if there be those who uphold this scripture, I shall guard and protect them and ward off their misfortunes. Whether such a person is walking or standing while reciting this sutra, at that time I shall mount my six-tusked white elephant and manifest before them together with a great host of bodhisattvas, to make offerings and give protection — for that is also to make offerings to the Lotus Sutra itself. Should they forget so much as a single line or verse of the sutra, I will teach it to them so that they remember it once more."

## Editor's Reflection

The "Encouragement" chapter closes the Lotus Sutra and was for centuries the chapter reciters held closest: the scripture itself promises that one who recites it in a defiled age is never alone. The many later records of "seeing the white elephant" or "being taught a forgotten line" all take root in this very passage. Rather than reading the response stories as isolated curiosities, the editor suggests reading them against this background: the compilers first had the sutra's promise, and out of that promise arose both their expectation and their record-keeping.

In the sutra's final chapter, Samantabhadra arrives from the eastern realm of the Buddha Jewel-Majesty-Virtue-Sovereign-King to ask how beings will obtain the sutra after the Tathāgata's passing. The Buddha names four dharmas; Samantabhadra then vows to protect upholders of the sutra in the later evil age, manifesting mounted on his six-tusked white elephant, and teaching back any line or verse they forget.
Samantabhadra Bodhisattva; Śākyamuni Buddha

普賢 遍吉 Samantabhadra32:Tbe3,# Kumārajīva: The Translator Whose Tongue Did Not Burn

> Recorded at the head of the "Eminent Monks" section of the *Xianying lu* and of the Jin-dynasty section of the *Chiyan ji* — both compilations place him first, treating him as the fountainhead of Lotus Sutra devotion. The English rendering follows the fuller *Xianying lu* text.

## The Story

The Dharma-master Kumārajīva — the name is rendered in Chinese as "Youthful Seniority" — was a native of Kucha. He left home at seven and could recite a thousand gāthās a day. His mother once took him to the land of the Yuezhi, where an arhat said of the boy: "If this novice keeps the precepts intact until the age of thirty-five, he will greatly spread the Buddha's teaching and liberate countless people; if his discipline fails, he will be merely a brilliant scholar of the Dharma."

His mother later told him: "The profound teaching of the Vaipulya ought to be greatly propagated in the Middle Land; that task depends on you alone — though it will bring no benefit to yourself." Kumārajīva replied: "The way of the great being is to benefit others and forget one's own body. If the great Dharma can be spread and wash clean the deluded world, then even were I to enter furnace and cauldron, I would have no regret."

Yao Xing of the Later Qin welcomed Kumārajīva to Chang'an with the honours due a royal preceptor. In the eighth year of the Hongshi era he and more than eight hundred clerics, led by Seng-lüe, collated the sutra against earlier versions in the Xiaoyao Garden's Caotang Monastery and translated the *Saddharma Puṇḍarīka Sūtra* (Lotus Sutra) in seven fascicles.

Falling ill near the end, Kumārajīva said to the assembly: "We met through the Dharma, and I have not fully given my heart; now the karmic conditions are ending. May the sutras I have translated — the Lotus above all — continue to circulate among you. If my translations contain no error, then after this body is burned, my tongue alone will not be scorched." With these words he passed away. When the body was cremated, the tongue indeed remained whole.

## Editor's Reflection

Every later account of Lotus Sutra "responses" rests on a single premise: that there exists a Lotus Sutra one can trust and recite. Kumārajīva's story recalls where that sutra came from — an act of linguistic conversion underwritten by an entire life. "The unburnt tongue" is a religious narrative, and also a solemn metaphor: fidelity in translation that outlasts death. Readers who use the two languages of this site are, in a small way, continuing that same work.

The Kuchean monk Kumārajīva, invited to Chang'an by Yao Xing, translated the Lotus Sutra at Caotang Monastery. Before dying he vowed that if his translations were without error, his tongue would remain unburnt after cremation — and so it proved. Both response-compilations used by this site open with him.
Kumārajīva

羅什 什法師 童壽 Kumārajīva Kumarajiva33:Tf9d,# Master Bacheng: Sixty Years of Recitation, a Single Prayer for the West

> Recorded in the "Eminent Monks" section of the *Xianying lu* under the heading "The Dharma-master Chen" (沙門澄法師) and again in the Jin-dynasty section of the *Chiyan ji* under "The monk Bacheng of Chang'an" (晉長安釋䟷澄). The surname character is printed in the sources with the rare variant 䟷 (CBETA character CB02494); this site follows the received form 跋澄 in running text. The translation follows the fuller *Xianying lu* version.

## The Story

Bacheng left home to become a monk at the age of twenty. His faculties were dull; when he first ordained he hardly knew what to do with himself. So he made a fixed resolve: he would recite the Lotus Sutra, and by this he would pray for rebirth in the Western Pure Land. Each day he could retain one line of the text, sometimes only half a verse. In this laborious way he practised until, at the age of eighty, he had completed the whole sutra.

One day, sitting in meditation, he suddenly saw a deity in vermilion robes holding a formal letter of invitation, which said: "The Lord of Heaven requests the presence of the Great Master Bacheng."

Bacheng replied: "I was born dull, and have merely recited according to my small capacity, praying solely for Ultimate Bliss. Trāyastriṃśā, though an exalted realm, is not what I aspire to." The vermilion-clad envoy thereupon withdrew.

At another time he dreamt of a great pagoda made of the seven jewels, himself standing on its fifth storey, gazing upon flights of stairs ascending a city of seven jewels, stretching on without end. Two vajra guardians stood outside bearing their pestles; several dozen attendants in blue held white chowries, sweeping the stairway. Bacheng asked what this was, and a voice answered: "This is the jeweled city of the West. The stairway has come to receive you."

Bacheng awoke from the dream and at once instructed his disciples: "Take my robes and alms-bowl, and prepare a communal offering for the assembly." Then he asked: "At this very moment, do you see the thousand Buddhas?" They answered: "We do not." He asked again: "Do you perceive the extraordinary fragrance?" They answered: "We all smell it." With these words he turned to face the west, and passed serenely away.

The *Xianying lu* notes at the end of the account that its source was the *Lingrui ji* (Collection of Auspicious Responses).

Bacheng may be the slowest practitioner in either compilation: sixty years for one sutra. Among this site's "Pure Land" stories it is the most direct record of vow and resolve.

## The Story

At Kaiyuan Monastery in Xiangzhou there was a master named Daoang, who regularly lectured on the Lotus Sutra.

The episode also appears in the *Platform Sutra of the Sixth Patriarch* with wording that differs in places (see the textual notes). The English rendering follows the *Chiyan ji* text.

## The Story

Fada, a native of Hongzhou, left home at seven and spent his years reciting the Lotus Sutra.

Later he went to Caoxi to pay homage to the Sixth Patriarch Huineng, who asked him: "You recite this sutra — what do you take as its purport?"

Fada said: "I only recite it by the letter; how would I know its purport?"

The Patriarch told him to fetch the sutra, unfold it and read. Fada read aloud as far as the Parable chapter when the Patriarch said: "Stop. This sutra takes 'appearing in the world for a cause' as its purport. The sutra says: 'I appeared in the world for one great matter.' That one great matter is the Buddha's insight and vision. If one can be free of appearances within appearances, free of emptiness within emptiness, and in a single thought the mind opens — that is opening the Buddha's insight and vision. Believe this: the Buddha's insight and vision is your own mind itself; there is no other thing besides. Do not seek outward — you are no different from the Buddha."

Then he gave a verse:

> When the mind is deluded, it is turned by the Lotus Sutra;
> when the mind awakens, it turns the Lotus Sutra.
> Reciting long without understanding, one makes an enemy of the meaning.
> In no-thought, recollection is right; in thought, recollection goes wrong.
> Neither counting "is" nor counting "is not",
> one drives the great white ox-cart forever.

Hearing this, Fada burst into tears, and answered with a verse of praise containing the line:

> Three thousand recitations of the sutra —
> undone by one sentence at Caoxi.

From that moment he awakened to the profound purport.

## Editor's Reflection

Among all the entries in the two compilations, this is the only one structured as a dialogue, and the only one adopted by the Chan school: Fada wins no auspicious sign by reciting — he recites without gaining power, and is awakened by a single sentence. "When the mind is deluded it is turned by the sutra; when it awakens, it turns the sutra": the sutra is still the same sutra; the person has changed his very mind, and subject and object have flipped entire.

The closing image repays attention. The Lotus Sutra uses the goat-cart, deer-cart and ox-cart as the three vehicles of inducement, and the great white ox-cart as the One Buddha-vehicle; the Patriarch says "neither counting 'is' nor counting 'is not', one drives the great white ox-cart forever" — only when even the calculations of being and non-being are dropped is one fit to ride that cart. It is a brilliant Chan re-reading of the sutra's parable. Three thousand recitations with the darkness unbroken — an "enemy of the meaning"; this site files the account under "Insight" precisely to recall that in the world of the response records, the rarest auspicious sign is the turning around.

"The snap of fingers in the dark" is the most delicate touch in this account: in the vocabulary of the response literature, a finger-snap is the sound of unseen protectors signalling approval — the quiet encouragement of ordinary nights preceding the hidden assistance in mortal danger, the two echoing like an acquaintance who has long since made himself known.

## The Story

He asked: "Do you too wish to cross?" The tigers rose at once, crossed the water together in safety, and reached the south bank. When he looked back, the little boat and the old man had vanished.

Thereafter Zhicong kept the four tigers with him on the west of Qixia Monastery on Mount She. In the Zhenguan era, ninety-nine years old, he died seated upright; the tigers went away with mournful roars.

## Editor's Reflection

The most moving episode is the one only the *Xianying lu* preserves — the offering of his body to the tigers: a monk seven days without food gives his body to the hungry beasts that guard him. The gesture inverts the usual structure of "recite and be saved": the one protected becomes the giver, and the protectors become the recipients. "The four tigers shed tears and would not leave" are the weightiest eight characters in the whole account.

In the editor's reading, the real "response" here is neither the tigers nor the boat, but the composure of a man who in a collapsing world still "felt no hunger for seven days".

## The Story

In the Eastern Jin there was a monk named Fazhi who built a hut in the Yuhang hills and recited the Lotus Sutra morning and evening without fail.

A pheasant nested beside the hut. Whenever it heard the sound of recitation it would fly down and gather at Fazhi's seat, standing by as though in attendance, listening — and so it did for seven years, without missing a day.

One day the pheasant suddenly drooped, near its end. Fazhi stroked it and said: "Though you are of the feathered kind, you have been able to listen to the sutra. If you can shed this karmic body, you shall surely be born in the human state."

The next morning the pheasant died. Fazhi buried it.

That night, as Fazhi lay in a light sleep, a boy appeared in a dream and bowed to him twice, saying: "I am that pheasant. Through listening to your recitation of the sutra I escaped the feathered kind, and I am now born a boy in the Wang family in front of the hill. Beneath my right arm there still remain the down-feathers of a pheasant — you may verify it there."

At daybreak Fazhi went to the Wang household to ask, and it was exactly so.

Later the Wang family held a vegetarian feast, and Fazhi came to the door; the moment the boy saw him he said: "My master has come!" The whole company marvelled. Fazhi stroked him and said: "This is my pheasant child." They opened his robe and examined beneath his arms — indeed there were three stalks of pheasant feather.

When the boy was seven, the time fit for ordination, his parents consented. He entered the mountains at the appointed time and took the tonsure at sixteen. Because of the feathers beneath his arm he was given the name Tanyi — "Wing". The Lotus Sutra was handed to him, and he retained it without missing a single word. After Fazhi passed away, Tanyi became the second patriarch of that mountain.

## Editor's Reflection

Of all the stories in this collection, this is the most complete closed loop on the merit of *listening* to the sutra: seven years of listening → the prophecy (the stroking and its words) → death → dream verification → birthmark proof (three feathers beneath the arm) → naming (Tanyi) → receiving the sutra without missing a word → succession as second patriarch. Every link carries an identifiable "physical exhibit" and "human witness" — which is precisely why such accounts circulated so durably.

"This is my pheasant child" is the emotional anchor of the whole piece — one sentence claiming back seven years of companionship. The reader need not press the question of historical factuality; as religious literature it expresses the ancients' deep conviction that "where the voice of the Dharma reaches, even other forms of life find liberation".

Tanyi, mindful of the impropriety, firmly refused. The woman wept and pleaded without cease. Reluctantly he allowed her a bed of grass apart from his own, and covered his head and recited the sutra.

Tanyi said: "I am a monk under the great precepts — to touch a woman's body, what manner of thing is that?" But the pleading was so urgent that he wrapped his staff's head in cloth and massaged her from a distance. After a little while the woman said: "It is no longer needed."

At dawn the woman stepped into the courtyard — her embroidered robes dissolved into auspicious cloud, the piglet turned into a white elephant, the garlic into twin lotuses. With her feet upon the lotuses, mounted on the elephant, riding the cloud, she said to Tanyi:

With these words she faded into the distance. Flowers rained from heaven and the earth trembled; the villagers who saw and heard could not withhold their praise.

That very morning the governor Meng Yi, at his desk since early dawn, saw in the south an auspicious cloud breathing light that shot across his courtyard, with the faint sound of chimes and strings beneath it. On inquiry he received the whole account of Samantabhadra's manifestation to Tanyi, and reported it to the throne together with the master's practice. An imperial decree established a monastery, titled "Fahua" — the Lotus — in the thirteenth year of Yixi of Emperor An of the Eastern Jin (417). That monastery is today's Tianyi Monastery.

Tanyi lived to seventy and passed away on that mountain. The community lacquered his true body and kept it there. Through the persecution of the Huichang era, when Buddhism was abolished, the monks hid his image in the hollow of a tree south of the monastery and it escaped destruction. ## Editor's Reflection

This is the most widely circulated account in this site's "peace in this life" theme, and the most vivid fulfilment of the "Encouragement of Samantabhadra" chapter (S002 in this collection): the sutra promises that one who upholds and recites it "whether walking or standing, I shall mount my six-tusked white elephant… and manifest to make offerings and protect" — what Tanyi received was precisely the white elephant's manifestation, though in the guise of a test.

The design of the test is exquisite: deep night, a lone mountain, a beautiful woman, the plea for lodging, the plea for massage — pressure layered upon pressure; and Tanyi's response has its own measure at every layer — the firm refusal, the separate bed, the covered head and recitation, the medicine, the cloth-wrapped staff. In the night he dreamt of a monk who told him: "Master Fayun was already lecturing this sutra in the days of Dipankara Buddha — how could you hope to rival him at a stroke?"

## Editor's Reflection

Fayun represents the summit of the art of sutra-lecturing in the Six Dynasties. The account sets three tiers of response to certify his lecturing: celestial flowers like snow (the sign within the lecture hall itself); rain at "its moisture pervades all" (the lecture's text answering the weather — a stroke of the highest literary craft: the rain falls precisely upon the phrase that speaks of moisture); and "he taught this sutra in the days of Dipankara Buddha" (tracing his lecturing career back through measureless kalpas, in tacit mirror of the Lotus Sutra's own opening scene, where the Buddha Dipankara expounds this very sutra).

"Clouds bring rain" is the poem at the heart of the entry — his own name become prophecy. And the scene of the solitary lecture — stones ranked as his assembly, a pine twig for his whisk — preserves for us the loneliest and most romantic portrait of the mountain sutra-master of antiquity. The entry contains no word of rebirth; this site files it under "peace in this life / auspicious signs".

Fayun of the Liang, of the Zhou family of Yangxian, was born with cloud-vapour filling the room, and ordained at seven. At thirty he lectured on the Lotus and the Vimalakīrti with dialectic like a rising wind, and students flocked to him; Emperor Wu of Liang placed him over Guangzhai Monastery. Alone in a secluded cliff he once lectured the sutra to rows of stones, breaking a pine twig for a whisk. Lecturing it, celestial flowers fell like flying snow filling the sky, drifted into the hall, hung in the air without falling, and departed only when the lecture ended. The master Zhi Gong begged him to expound the 'lion's roar', and snapped his fingers in praise at the telling. When the emperor prayed for rain during a drought, Zhi Gong advised summoning Fayun; lecturing, at the phrase 'its moisture pervades all' the sky poured down its bounty. At Yuan Ang's home a resident monk vowed to attain wisdom like Fayun's, and dreamt of a monk who told him: Master Fayun was already lecturing this sutra in the days of Dipankara Buddha — how could you hope to rival him at a stroke? The source cites the Xu Gaoseng zhuan.
