19:Tae5,# Master Kejiu: Names on the Lotus Platforms, Verified Five Years On

> Recorded in the Song-dynasty section of the *Chiyan ji*, juan 2, under "The monk Kejiu of the Song". The *Xianying lu*, compiled in the Song, does not carry him (its coverage ends with figures before the Southern Song). The English rendering follows the *Chiyan ji*.

## The Story

Master Kejiu lived at Kaiyuan Monastery in Siming, constantly reciting the Lotus Sutra, and people called him "Master Jiu of the Lotus". His closest friendship was with Su Shi (the posthumously honoured "Wenzhong"); he lived simply at Xiangfu Monastery by West Lake, one bare room, poor and incorruptible.

One Lantern Festival, Su Shi had been viewing the lanterns at the Nine Bend; his attendants had scattered, and walking alone he came to Kejiu's room — no lamp burned within, only the lingering scent of gardenia. Su Shi gazed up and sighed, leaving behind a poem containing the lines: "He hangs no glass to idle-light the Buddha — now I know the inexhaustible was never lamp."

In the eighth Yuanyou year (1093) Kejiu died seated, without illness. After three days he revived and recounted: "In spirit I travelled to the Pure Land and saw that realm with my own eyes; its adornments are exactly as the Sixteen Contemplations Sutra describes. Upon all the lotus platforms of the seven-jeweled pool are written the names of those yet to be born there. One platform of purple gold bore the name of the monk Xun of Guangjiao Monastery in Chengdu — he has already been born upon it. Another of gold bore Sun the Twelfth of Mingzhou; one bore Kejiu. And one of silver bore the nun Xu of Mingzhou."

With that he passed away once more.

Five years later the nun Xu died, extraordinary fragrance filling her room; seven years after that Sun the Twelfth passed away amid celestial music sounding through the sky — each one fulfilled exactly as Kejiu had said.

## Editor's Reflection

The structure of this record is rare in the response literature: the protagonist himself revives to testify, and his "predictions" are then confirmed, one by one, by the auspicious deaths of others five and seven years later. In preserving him, the compiler in effect preserved three rebirth records at once. His life is noted only for Lotus Sutra recitation — no details of Pure Land practice are recorded — yet everything he reports conforms to the Pure Land imagery of the Contemplation Sutra. The editor files him under the Pure Land theme as *interpreted*, and marks his vow status "uncertain", since the source records no vow in his own words.

Worth noting in passing: Su Shi's encounter with the reciting monk — the sigh over "the inexhaustible lamp" — is a vivid vignette of how Song literati engaged with Lotus Sutra devotion.
