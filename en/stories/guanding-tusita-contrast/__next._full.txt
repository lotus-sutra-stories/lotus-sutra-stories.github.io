34:T1028,# Master Guanjing: A Lotus Sutra Career, a Recorded Tuṣita Destination

> Recorded in the "Eminent Monks" section of the *Xianying lu* under "Chan Master Zongchi of Zhang'an", citing Gu Shan's *Xianju bian*; Zongchi ("Total Retention") is the posthumous title conferred by the King of Wuyue. **This entry is the deliberate contrast case in this site's Pure Land theme: the source records his destination as the Tuṣita heaven, not Amitābha's Western Pure Land.** The English rendering follows the *Xianying lu*.

## The Story

Master Guanjing, lay surname Wu, was a native of Zhang'an. At three years old, still an infant, he could join his mother in taking refuge in the Three Jewels; all marvelled, and he was given the childhood name "Bufan" — "Out of the Ordinary". After entering the Way he could record ten thousand words a day, and pursued metaphysics and the classics alike. He received the Tiantai teaching of meditative concentration and wisdom joined, and continued Zhiyi's work as an heir — whatever the Great Master taught, Guanjing wrote down.

In later years he settled at Chengxin Hermitage and lectured on the Lotus Sutra at length; his teaching flowed among the clamorous laity, and his uncanny efficacy knew no fixed measure. A man of Falong village, living thirty li off in the hills, had fallen mortally ill, and his son ran to beg for help. For him Guanjing recited the Lotus Sutra and burned sandalwood: the sick man, thirty li away, smelled the fragrance from afar — and recovered.

At South Ridge in Le'an there is a place called Anzhou, emerald trees and clear streams, where no human path leads. Guanjing went to look, lingered, and could not leave; so he swore a vow: "If this ground may be made level, I will come here and lecture the Lotus Sutra." Before ten days had passed, white sand welled up everywhere, level as a jade mirror. He fulfilled his former vow and went to teach there.

He was also once lecturing on the Nirvana Sutra at Shejing Monastery when sea-raiders came to plunder. Guanjing struck the bell and lectured as usual. The brigands entered the monastery — and saw war-banners blazing in the sun, giants a zhang and more in height bearing bows and halberds, whereupon they broke and scattered.

In the sixth Zhenguan year (632), at Guoqing Monastery, he fell ill. He told his disciples: "The Maitreya Sutra says: on the day the Buddha enters extinction, the incense-smoke is like clouds. Burn much incense — I am going." With these words he passed away; the room filled with extraordinary fragrance.

Years before this, the Chan master Zhixi — who had died early in the first Zhenguan year — had told the assembly at his passing: "I have been born in Tuṣita. I saw the Great Master Zhiyi seated upon a jeweled seat, with a ranked row of seats; one seat alone stood empty. A deva said: 'Six years hence, Master Guanjing ascends to teach here.'" Counting the years and the term, Zhixi's words had proven true to the day.

## Editor's Reflection

Guanjing is a figure no history of Lotus Sutra devotion can bypass — the three great Tiantai treatises exist because of his brush. This site includes him not only for those characteristic this-life responses (the remote healing, the welling sand, the giants that routed the raiders), but for a classificatory reason as well: **the source plainly records him going to Tuṣita, not to the Western Pure Land.** His own deathbed quotation is from the Maitreya Sutra, and the prediction that precedes it names Tuṣita — evidence that within the world of these compilations, the Lotus practitioner's destination at death had more than one option.

That is precisely why this site separates "auspicious signs" from "recorded destination". A reader who takes every fragrance and every celestial music as "rebirth in Sukhāvatī" will miss what the sources actually record. Guanjing and Daoang (S007 in this collection) are mirror images: one received a Tuṣita prediction and followed it; one declined the heavens and chose the West. Read side by side, they display the real spectrum of Pure Land aspiration before the Tang.
