# Master Fayun: Flowers Rain on the Lecture; He Taught This Sutra in the Days of Dipankara Buddha

> Recorded in the "Eminent Monks" section of the *Xianying lu* under "Master Yun of Jinling" (citing the *Xu Gaoseng zhuan*), and again in the Liang section of the *Chiyan ji* under "The monk Fayun of the Liang". The English rendering follows the fuller *Xianying lu*.

## The Story

Master Fayun was a native of Yangxian, lay surname Zhou. When his mother bore him, cloud-vapour filled the room, and so "Cloud" became his childhood name. He ordained at seven under the name Fayun — Dharma Cloud — brilliant and outstanding beyond his generation. Master Liang, seeing him, exclaimed: "My own spirit and wisdom truly fall short of this boy's; this child will be a pillar of the great Dharma."

At thirty Fayun began lecturing on the Lotus Sutra and the Vimalakīrti Sutra; his dialectic rose like wind, and students converged on him from all quarters. Emperor Wu of Liang honoured him with the charge of Guangzhai Monastery.

From his days as a novice he had loved the Lotus Sutra with a scholar's devotion, refining his thought upon it for years. Once he went into a secluded ravine and lectured the sutra entirely alone — setting out stones in rows for his assembly, breaking a pine twig for his whisk, intoning and expounding by himself, answering his own difficult questions; and his commentaries layered upon one another, illuminating text after text.

On another occasion, lecturing the sutra at a monastery, he suddenly drew down celestial flowers — like flying snow they filled the whole sky, drifted into the hall, hung in the air without falling, and dispersed only when the lecture was done.

At that time the master Zhi Gong, whose way transcended the world, would sometimes come to Fayun and say: "I should like to ask the master to expound the 'lion's roar'." Fayun would ascend the seat and dissect the theme, and Zhi Gong would snap his fingers in praise: "Excellent — how subtle!"

One day, during a drought, Emperor Wu asked Zhi Gong: "What means can bring rain?" Zhi Gong said: "Clouds bring rain — summon Master Yun to lecture." Fayun lectured the Lotus, and when he reached the phrase "its moisture pervades all" — the sky broke open in a great pouring rain.

At the house of Yuan Ang, Commander of the Guard, there was a monk long supported by the family who vowed to attain wisdom-understanding equal to Fayun's. In the night he dreamt of a monk who told him: "Master Fayun was already lecturing this sutra in the days of Dipankara Buddha — how could you hope to rival him at a stroke?"

## Editor's Reflection

Fayun represents the summit of the art of sutra-lecturing in the Six Dynasties. The account sets three tiers of response to certify his lecturing: celestial flowers like snow (the sign within the lecture hall itself); rain at "its moisture pervades all" (the lecture's text answering the weather — a stroke of the highest literary craft: the rain falls precisely upon the phrase that speaks of moisture); and "he taught this sutra in the days of Dipankara Buddha" (tracing his lecturing career back through measureless kalpas, in tacit mirror of the Lotus Sutra's own opening scene, where the Buddha Dipankara expounds this very sutra).

"Clouds bring rain" is the poem at the heart of the entry — his own name become prophecy. And the scene of the solitary lecture — stones ranked as his assembly, a pine twig for his whisk — preserves for us the loneliest and most romantic portrait of the mountain sutra-master of antiquity. The entry contains no word of rebirth; this site files it under "peace in this life / auspicious signs".
