# Yan Gong: Making Sutra Copies with Care

> This editorial rendering follows the cited source. Extraordinary events are reported as religious narratives, not independently established facts.

## The account

Yan Gong, a man from Danyang during the Sui period, practised Buddhism with his household and regularly recited the Lotus Sutra. The text says that, after reading the chapter on the appearance of the treasure tower, he longed to see the Buddhas described there. In a dream, a monk calling himself Fatuo encouraged him to explain, copy, circulate, and make offerings to the sutra.

Yan first vowed to produce a hundred copies. Before completing them, he became seriously ill and increased his vow to a thousand. The account then reports his recovery and the establishment of a copying hall in his home in Yangdu. He was to obtain paper and brushes honestly and with a sincere mind. More than ten scribes regularly worked there; he supplied what they needed and personally checked their work without shrinking from the effort.

The same entry relates religious accounts of lost funds returning, an unusual monk donating gold, supernatural assistance, and a sutra case found floating on the water. It says that the undertaking eventually reached three thousand copies. These are the compilation’s reports, not independently audited financial or production figures.

Yan later vowed that no character would escape his eyes or his attention. According to the source, his descendants continued the work and he became known as “Gong of the Lotus Sutra.” It also reports that bandits in the final Sui years agreed not to enter his neighbourhood. Within this religious account, the copying hall makes visible the sustained work of provision, checking, and circulation.

## Editorial reflection (not source text)

Supporting a text can include careful reading, checking, and helping others obtain copies. A small, attentive task is a reasonable starting point today; the source’s large numbers need not become a measure of personal achievement.
