# Zhu Ruyi: Ten Years Embroidering the Lotus Sutra

> This editorial rendering follows the cited source. Extraordinary events are reported as religious narratives, not independently established facts.

## The account

Zhu Ruyi was married into the Xue household in Siming. The Xianying lu says that, in her twenties, she told her husband of her wish to live in purity, wearing plain clothes and observing a disciplined life. She commissioned a scribe to write the seven scrolls of the Lotus Sutra on yellow silk, then embroidered the characters herself with blue-green silk thread. The work took ten years.

The entry also describes embroidered images of Amitabha and Avalokiteshvara, reverence for the sutra’s title, learning to recite the text from memory, and encouraging others to recite Amitabha’s name. Its figures of two hundred thousand people and large assemblies are retained as claims within the account, not independently established statistics.

In the Xianying lu version, Zhu tried to sit cross-legged during a slight illness near death. Her husband asked her to lie down, and she died on her right side, aged thirty-seven. The Chiyan ji preserves a shorter account with additional details: relics reportedly appearing at her needle, a date in the fourth Shaoxi year, and her own declaration that rebirth was assured. These details belong to their respective witnesses; they are not combined into a fabricated common original.

Her Amitabha practice makes the story relevant to Pure Land reading. However, a declaration made before death and the posture at death are not a narrator’s independent confirmation of rebirth in Sukhāvatī. This draft therefore retains an interpreted classification and the existing policy of marking translations as editorial drafts awaiting independent review.

## Editorial reflection (not source text)

Long-term craft and sustained attention can be more accessible points of reflection than large numbers. Writing, organizing materials, or helping circulation can be adapted to ability. Years of embroidery, worship counts, and responses to illness are not measures of another person’s faith.
