# Master Fache: Teaching the Sutra Line by Line, the Leper's Skin Made Whole

> Recorded in the Tang section of the *Chiyan ji*, juan 1, under "The monk Fache of Puzhou". The *Xianying lu* does not carry it. The English rendering follows the *Chiyan ji*.

## The Story

Master Fache was a native of Jiangzhou. From youth he applied himself with hardship, mastered the Lotus Sutra, and spent his life drawing and converting people by means of that sutra alone.

Within the borders of Puzhou there is a lone hill. Fache settled there, building practice upon the mountain, and founded a hermitage on the spot. The site was high, steep and dry, altogether without a spring; but after years of devoted practice, one day a clear fountain burst up from the ground. The prefect Fang Renyu petitioned for an imperial plaque for the monastery, which was named "Sunken Spring".

Once, on the road, Fache came upon a man afflicted with leprosy. He led the sick man into the hills, dug out a cave dwelling for him, supplied his food, and set him to reciting the Lotus Sutra.

The leper had never learned to read, and he was dull of nature besides; so Fache taught him phrase by phrase, line upon line, and never once wearied of it.

By the time he had recited through the sixth fascicle, the disease was visibly receding. When the whole sutra was complete, eyebrows and beard had grown again, and his skin was restored as before.

## Editor's Reflection

The centre of this account is not the bursting spring (that is the "infrastructure miracle" by which the hermitage won official recognition), but the single phrase "taught him line upon line, never once wearying". An illiterate and dull-witted leper, from the first phrase to the end of the seventh fascicle — every line of it taught to him by Fache's own mouth: this is a rare record of an actual teaching scene within the response literature.

The relations among sutra, patient and teacher repay reflection too: the timetable of the cure advances in step with the timetable of the recitation (by the sixth fascicle, healing; at the sutra's end, skin whole) — this "quantified" narrative is precisely how pre-modern religious literature built its credibility. As for whether this constitutes a cure in the sense of modern medicine, this site, in accordance with its disclaimer, presents the account as religious literature and makes no medical claim.
